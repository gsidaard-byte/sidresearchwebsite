# Students at the Center: Research, Confidence, Service, and Growth

Students come to aerospace and engineering with different levels of experience, confidence, and certainty about what comes next. This community is built to help students grow through hands-on learning, meaningful mentoring, shared problem-solving, and service to others. The goal is not simply to complete projects. It is to help students develop the confidence to ask questions, test ideas, contribute to a team, and see themselves as engineers whose work can serve the common good.

## What students gain

- **Confidence through practice:** Students build confidence by explaining ideas in their own words, revising their work, and seeing how concepts connect over time.
- **Hands-on learning that feels real:** Students work with tools, experiments, simulations, and design challenges that make aerospace concepts tangible.
- **Mentoring and individual attention:** Students are known by name, supported through questions, and encouraged to keep going when material feels difficult.
- **Research experience with purpose:** Students can contribute to projects, presentations, theses, and publications while learning how research is done step by step.
- **Leadership and belonging:** Students learn in teams, take on responsibility in clubs, and become part of a community that values contribution, not competition.
- **Service and outward impact:** Students are invited to use what they learn in ways that support younger learners, the local community, and the future of engineering.

## How we build confidence

Confidence grows when students are challenged and supported at the same time. The evidence in the teaching materials points to a few practices that help students build that confidence in plain, practical ways.

Students are supported early. In class, students are not expected to arrive already knowing how to do everything. Learning is scaffolded so that each week builds on the last, helping students see progress instead of feeling lost in isolated topics.

Students are given room to think out loud. Small-group discussion, collaborative problem solving, and portfolio-based reflection give students a way to test ideas, ask questions, and learn without the pressure of performing perfectly in public. That matters for students who are still finding their voice.

Students learn by doing. Wind tunnel work, flight simulation, design-build-fly activities, coding, and project-based assignments help students connect theory to action. For many students, this is where aerospace becomes real and where confidence begins to stick.

Students are invited to reflect on their growth. Portfolio-style work, guided notes, and instructor reflection make learning visible. Students can look back, see what they understand now that they did not understand before, and recognize that progress is happening.

Students are treated as people, not just course rosters. The evidence repeatedly points to a caring, responsive environment where students feel seen, supported, and encouraged to keep growing.

## Research experiences

Research experiences are available to students at different stages, including undergraduate researchers, honors thesis students, independent study students, master's students, and doctoral students.

Students may:

- work on hands-on experimental projects in aerodynamics, propulsion, flow diagnostics, simulation, or related areas
- contribute to wind tunnel, water tunnel, and flight simulator projects
- join summer research experiences or independent studies
- complete honors theses, master's theses, or dissertation work
- present at local and regional conferences
- contribute to publications and longer-term research efforts

These experiences are not reserved only for students who already see themselves as researchers. Students can enter at different points, learn through guided practice, and grow into larger responsibilities over time.

Research also helps students connect classroom learning to professional identity. Students learn how to ask better questions, work through uncertainty, collaborate with others, communicate technical ideas, and stay with a problem long enough to produce something meaningful.

Outcomes may include presentations, theses, design experience, technical communication, and a stronger sense of direction for graduate study, industry, or other next steps. Specific outcomes depend on the project and the student's stage of involvement.

## Service and student clubs

Student clubs create an important bridge between coursework and belonging. They give students a place to practice leadership, build friendships, apply technical skills, and experience aerospace as a shared effort.

Students can find community and responsibility through clubs such as:

- **Design Build Fly (DBF):** Students design, build, and fly aircraft for mission-driven challenges. The work invites persistence, creativity, and teamwork.
- **IT Flies:** Students test ideas in flight simulation, receive feedback, and develop confidence by seeing designs and decisions play out in a realistic environment.
- **UD AIAA and related aerospace activities:** Students connect with guest speakers, outreach events, design projects, and the broader aerospace community.

These spaces matter because students are not only building technical skill. They are also learning how to contribute to a team, lead a project, support peers, and stay engaged when the work becomes demanding.

Service is part of that experience too. Students are encouraged to participate in outreach, open houses, engineering events, and community-facing activities that remind them engineering is not only about solving problems, but also about serving people.

## Summer outreach: Air Camp

Air Camp helps middle school and younger students imagine themselves in aerospace before they have to decide whether they belong there.

At the University of Dayton, Air Camp activities have included experiences such as:

- wind tunnel demonstrations and hands-on learning
- flight simulator activities
- glider building and related design experiences
- exposure to aerospace ideas in ways that are active, accessible, and memorable

These experiences matter because they make engineering feel reachable. Students do not just hear about aerospace. They get to see it, touch it, test it, and ask questions in an environment designed to spark curiosity.

Air Camp also gives current UD students an opportunity to serve. Students can help facilitate activities, support younger learners, and practice leadership by turning their own knowledge into encouragement for someone else.

For families and future students, that kind of outreach sends an important message: aerospace learning can begin with curiosity, and students do not have to wait until college to feel welcomed into it.
