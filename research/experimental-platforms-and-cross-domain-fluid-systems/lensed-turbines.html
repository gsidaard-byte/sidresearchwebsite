Research Subcategory

Lensed Turbines

Experimental Platforms and Cross-Domain Fluid Systems / Lensed Turbines

This research examines how wind-lens geometries modify turbine performance and how neighboring lenses interact aerodynamically when placed in close proximity. The work combines design, small-scale performance testing, wake measurements, and array-level thinking to understand how ducted or flanged turbine concepts behave as aerodynamic systems rather than isolated devices. Wake skew, vortex interaction, spacing effects, and proximity-dependent power changes all play a central role. The studies show that the value of a lensed turbine is not only in local augmentation, but in how the lens shapes the wake environment seen by nearby devices.

Why It Matters

Wind-energy performance is strongly influenced by wake behavior, yet many designs are still evaluated primarily in isolation. Lensed turbines bring that issue into sharper focus because the surrounding geometry can intensify beneficial flow features while also creating strong interaction effects in arrays. Understanding those interactions matters for compact energy systems, distributed deployment, and any configuration where multiple devices share limited space. The broader significance lies in showing that power production, wake merging, and vortex organization cannot be separated; performance depends on the aerodynamic relationship between neighboring units as much as on the efficiency of a single rotor.

Research Focus

The work spans both design optimization and wake physics. One study develops and tests a small-scale low-Reynolds-number wind lens turbine, maps proximity effects in multiple directions, and connects rotor performance to the behavior of nearby lenses and surrogate disk experiments. Another uses PIV and hot-wire measurements to show that close-spacing lens pairs develop an inherent wake bias, with one side of the pair carrying a stronger momentum deficit and thicker wake than the other. The journal study extends that analysis across several lens geometries and shows that at very close spacings the individual wakes merge, vortex shedding weakens, and net power can improve at the same spacing where wake interaction is strongest. The emphasis throughout is on how lens geometry and spacing reorganize vorticity, wake position, and power production in coupled turbine systems.
