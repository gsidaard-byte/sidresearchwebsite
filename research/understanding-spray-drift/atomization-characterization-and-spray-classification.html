Atomization Characterization and Spray Classification

Understanding Spray Drift / Atomization Characterization and Spray Classification

This research examines how agricultural sprays are formed at the moment of atomization, where nozzle geometry, operating pressure, and liquid formulation determine the droplet population that enters the surrounding flow. Using shadowgraph imaging and data-driven interpretation, the work studies how sprays from standard ASABE nozzle classes change across coarse-to-fine atomization regimes and how those changes can be recognized from the spray structure itself. A central contribution is showing that image-based methods can recover meaningful information about atomization state, including nozzle class and pressure condition, without relying exclusively on expensive laboratory droplet-sizing systems.

Why It Matters

Spray drift begins long before droplets move through a field or encounter ambient wind. It begins at the nozzle, in the size distribution and structure of the spray that is created in the first place. Because fine droplets are far more vulnerable to off-target transport, evaporation, and crop damage, the ability to characterize atomization quickly and reliably has direct importance for safer chemical application. This work also matters from a practical engineering standpoint because it points toward low-cost, adaptable diagnostic tools that can keep pace with changing nozzle designs, adjuvant mixtures, and real-world operating conditions.

Research Focus

The studies focus on extracting physically meaningful spray information from optical measurements of atomized plumes. Convolutional neural networks are used to distinguish nozzle types, chemical conditions, and pressure settings from shadowgraph images, including images captured with low-cost cameras. The broader aim is not simply classification for its own sake, but the development of a more accessible framework for linking visible spray structure to droplet-size behavior and drift risk. That makes atomization characterization more responsive to modern agricultural practice, where formulations and application conditions change faster than conventional reference models can easily accommodate.
