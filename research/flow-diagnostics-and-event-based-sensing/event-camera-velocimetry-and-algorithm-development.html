Event-Camera Velocimetry and Algorithm Development

Flow Diagnostics and Event-Based Sensing / Event-Camera Velocimetry and Algorithm Development

Research in this area develops event-based cameras into serious tools for experimental fluid mechanics rather than treating them as a novelty in machine vision. Unlike conventional imaging systems that record full frames at fixed time intervals, event cameras register changes in intensity asynchronously, making them especially attractive for flows with fast transients, sparse motion cues, or strong local gradients. The work spans particle event velocimetry, weighted tracking, Kalman-filter-based tracking, motion-compensation methods, simulator-based benchmarking, and sensitivity analysis across wing, wake, and cavity flows. Taken together, the studies show how event-driven sensing can recover meaningful velocity fields while preserving the experimental simplicity that makes planar diagnostics practical in the laboratory.

Why It Matters

Modern flow diagnostics are often constrained by the tradeoff between temporal resolution, data volume, and cost. Event-based sensing changes that tradeoff. By focusing only on changes in the scene rather than storing redundant image frames, it becomes possible to capture rapid motion with microsecond-scale timing, reduce data burden, and resolve localized structure that can be blurred or averaged out in frame-based approaches. That matters for unsteady aerodynamics, separated flows, wake measurements, and other regimes where the most important physics may be short-lived, spatially sharp, or difficult to isolate with conventional PIV workflows. The broader contribution is not only a new sensor, but a measurement philosophy better matched to highly dynamic flows.

Research Focus

The central effort here is to make event-based velocimetry reliable, interpretable, and experimentally usable. One line of work compares processing pipelines around an SD7003 wing and shows how different algorithms reproduce global trends, wake asymmetry, and near-surface gradients with different levels of bias and robustness. Another pushes toward high-density, noise-robust particle event velocimetry and uses synthetic benchmarking to test how well methods recover motion boundaries and curved flow structures. A further step introduces causal Kalman-filter tracking to enable streaming, computationally efficient reconstruction, while sensitivity studies identify how camera bias settings, threshold choices, and segmentation decisions affect accuracy, computation time, and performance across both fast outer flow and slow recirculating regions. There is also an adjacent manufacturing-flow study that broadens the application space by using event-based PIV to examine extraction flow in laser ablation environments.
