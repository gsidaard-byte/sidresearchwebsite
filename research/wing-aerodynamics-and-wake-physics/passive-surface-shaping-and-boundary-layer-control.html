Passive Surface Shaping and Boundary-Layer Control

Wing Aerodynamics and Wake Physics / Passive Surface Shaping and Boundary-Layer Control

This research explores how carefully chosen passive geometric changes can reshape the surface flow and wake of a wing without relying on powered actuation. The work spans segmented trailing-edge extensions, chordwise slots, curved boundary-layer fences, surface contours, and airfoil-preserved undulations, all aimed at altering how vorticity forms, convects, and organizes itself around the wing. Taken together, the studies show that passive modifications can influence both parasite drag and induced drag by changing separation behavior, spanwise flow feeding into the wingtip vortex, and the structure of the downstream wake.

Why It Matters

Passive aerodynamic control remains attractive because it offers a path to performance improvement without the added complexity, weight, and power demands of active systems. But passive devices only become useful when they target real flow mechanisms rather than serving as generic attachments. This body of work matters because it treats geometry as a way to redirect momentum and vorticity with purpose. By showing how different design features influence vortex shedding, free-shear-layer behavior, and wingtip-vortex strength, the research helps clarify when a passive change is genuinely aerodynamic and when it is merely cosmetic.

Research Focus

The studies focus on the relationship between local surface shaping and global wake behavior. Chordwise slots introduce streamwise momentum through the wing and alter the interaction between the free shear layer and the wingtip vortex. Surface contours and curved fences are used to redirect spanwise flow and weaken the feeding of the tip vortex. Segmented trailing-edge extensions change vortex shedding characteristics and reduce turbulent wake intensity, while airfoil-preserved undulations examine how maintaining the airfoil section while redistributing geometry along the span affects both lift and wake structure. Across these configurations, the central question is how passive geometry can produce repeatable changes in separation, turbulence, and vortex evolution that translate into meaningful aerodynamic gains.
