Research Subcategory

Variable Pitch Propeller

Propeller Aerodynamics and Distributed Propulsion / Variable Pitch Propeller

This research studies variable collective pitch propellers as active aerodynamic devices rather than fixed-thrust components. The work examines how blade pitch, incidence angle, and flight speed combine to shape thrust, power, and moment generation in forward flight and near-edgewise flight. Across the studies, variable pitch emerges as a way to adapt propeller behavior to changing operating conditions, especially when the rotor is no longer aligned with a simple axial inflow.

Why It Matters

Vehicles that must hover, transition, maneuver aggressively, or tolerate off-design flight conditions need propulsion systems that remain usable across very different inflow states. The experiments show that pitch scheduling can improve thrust predictability and extend useful operating range in axial and near-axial forward flight, but they also show the limits of that benefit. In hover, thrust varies approximately linearly with collective pitch over a useful range before stall appears. In axial forward flight, higher collective pitch can improve performance at higher speed, while in near-edgewise flight the efficiency benefit largely disappears even though control authority remains valuable. That distinction matters because it separates variable pitch as a control and operability tool from variable pitch as a guaranteed efficiency gain.

Research Focus

The work relies on six-component force and moment measurements across broad combinations of advance ratio, incidence angle, and blade pitch. One emphasis is prediction: the studies explore how thrust, pitching moment, and yawing moment can be normalized or curve-fit across operating states so that performance can be estimated beyond a single test point. Another emphasis is vehicle relevance, especially the question of whether a rotor-borne aircraft should tilt forward in flight or maintain a more edgewise condition while using other means to overcome in-plane drag. Taken together, the research builds a more complete picture of how variable-pitch propellers behave as controllable actuators in non-ideal flight regimes.
