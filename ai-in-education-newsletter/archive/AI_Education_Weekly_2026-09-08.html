Four developments that change what a professor does on Monday.

🎓 Higher Ed · 🏛️ Policy · Sept 2–3

Harvard's dean wants faculty "out of the AI-detection business"; the humanities are not ready to follow

In a first-day-of-classes email, Harvard College Dean David Deming said take-home exams and problem sets are "no longer a sustainable way to assess mastery," and proposed shifting to proctored exams while accepting or encouraging AI on papers too large for an exam window. He plans College-wide advisory guidelines by year's end, calling the email "initial thoughts," not policy.

The reception split along divisional lines. English professor Deirdre Lynch will keep a blanket AI ban in both her fall courses; historian Peter Gordon warned the approach would push students to outsource their thinking. Comparative literature professor Homi Bhabha will keep requiring AI logs but bars AI-written essays: "AI can help you prepare the ground for your thinking. But once the ground is prepared, you have to stand on it with your own voice."

Worth noting: Last week we reported Nature's finding that a new detector generation is far more accurate. Deming's argument does not rest on accuracy — and neither does Mount Royal University's August advisory, which recommends disabling Turnitin's detector on evidentiary and equity grounds, citing Washington State University's finding that 33% of AI-related integrity cases from 2023–2025 were dismissed because a detector score was the only evidence. What would resolve it: a published false-positive rate for the new detectors on writing by non-native English speakers.

What this means for your campus

If your integrity process still leans on a detector score, this week gave your provost two independent reasons to ask why — and neither of them is about whether the tool works.

🎓 Higher Ed · Sept 1–4

Blue books are back, and Nature catalogued what professors do instead of policing

Follett reports exam-booklet sales up nearly 40% since fall 2023; Roaring Spring, the last blue-book maker, is above pre-pandemic levels. Nature separately surveyed educators worldwide: pass/fail take-home work backed by calculator-only finals (Eindhoven), oral defense of every line of code (San José State), revision-history submissions instead of PDFs (MIT), grade caps when students can't explain their work (Princeton). Berkeley's Armando Fox calls handwritten exams "a retreat" and points to a proctored digital testing center instead.

What this means for your campus: The choice this fall is not paper versus screen — it is whether the graded moment happens where you can watch the student think.

🌍 Global · Sept 2

Australia: 2,000 Sydney staff strike over AI protections as universities split on AI-assisted grading

NTEU members at the University of Sydney went on strike for 24 hours seeking legally binding clauses that AI will not be used to cut jobs or replace human interaction in teaching. Branch president Peter Chen said management had rebuffed AI protections "at every opportunity." The AFR reports top research universities bar AI-assisted grading while Adelaide, Newcastle, Deakin and RMIT permit it with assessor oversight. Macquarie replaced weekly psychology tutorials in two units with its "Virtual Peer" chatbot; the university says it supplements rather than replaces teaching.

What this means for your campus: In Australia the AI fight has moved from academic integrity to the bargaining table. If your faculty agreement is silent on job security and on grading, this is what the next negotiation looks like.

🏫 K-12 · 🏛️ Policy · Sept 1–3

New York and Los Angeles paused student-facing AI for younger grades in the same 48 hours

New York City announced a one-year moratorium on student-facing generative AI for 2-K through 8th grade, covering nearly 600,000 students, plus a companion-chatbot ban in all grades and screen-time caps. Teachers may still use AI for planning. High schoolers get two 45-minute AI critical-thinking modules a year, and five vetted pilots — Quill, Edia, Brisk Teaching, Playlab and Intel AI-Ready Schools — reach a maximum of 50,000 students, roughly 5% of enrolment. A new Technology in Schools Coalition will review every tool in the system and bar anything not "mission-critical to learning."

A day later, Los Angeles Unified board members learned at a committee meeting that administrators had already imposed a district-wide student AI moratorium on their own authority; board member Nick Melvoin said he was "of two minds" that the step did not come from the board. Both decisions land weeks after Google switched Gemini on in Google Classroom for students of all ages — a rollout MIT's Justin Reich said forces districts to manage change "whenever the tech industry decides we have to deal with it."

What this means for your campus: Students arriving from these two systems will have had structured AI literacy but very little hands-on AI use — the opposite profile of the incoming cohorts many universities are now building literacy courses for.

Research & Practice

What the evidence says,
and who is already acting on it.

Two studies worth ten minutes, and two classrooms worth copying.

From the Research

Policy Analysis · Heliyon

Australasian universities regulate AI through integrity policies that never mention integrity

What was studied: Ann Dadich and Subas Dhakal combined a bibliometric review of 91 articles, content analysis of public policies at 49 Australian and New Zealand universities, and lexical analysis of the policy texts.

What they found: Only 22 of 49 universities explicitly mention generative AI, and only one — the University of Technology Sydney — has a dedicated AI policy. Most take a "moderative" stance, conditionally permitting use, but only Griffith and the University of Western Australia say how to acknowledge it. "Integrity" never clustered strongly enough to form a theme.

What you could do with this: Run the same two tests on your own policy — does it state what counts as acknowledgement, and does it define integrity beyond "do not submit AI output as your own"?

Survey · Frontiers in Education

In a 980-student study, transparency norms predicted ethical AI use more than technical skill did

What was studied: A cross-sectional survey of 980 students at the State University of Milagro, Ecuador, using a 44-item questionnaire and structural equation modeling.

What they found: Academic integrity and transparency showed the strongest association with ethical use (β = 0.231), ahead of ethical-technical literacy (0.181), critical verification (0.177), institutional guidance (0.159), self-regulation (0.119) and data protection (0.114). No single factor explained ethical use alone. Single-site purposive sampling limits how far this generalises.

What you could do with this: If you have one hour of AI instruction to spend, the data argue for disclosure norms and verification habits over tool tutorials.

How Others Are Doing It

University of Toronto Scarborough

A sociologist replaced the Wealth of Nations essay with a marketplace of AI agents

Who: Daniel Silver, sociologist, University of Toronto Scarborough (Canada).

What they did: Instead of an essay on Adam Smith, students built a virtual market of AI agents acting as traders, ran scenarios such as what happens when agents become untrustworthy, then analyzed the transcripts and wrote about them.

What happened: Students who asked an AI to do all the steps in one go were easy to spot, because the output invented material instead of quoting the transcripts.

What you could borrow: Anchor the written product to an artefact the student generated, so the evidence of thinking is the specific transcript rather than the prose style.

Source →
Eindhoven University of Technology

A computer scientist made take-home work pass/fail and put the grade in a calculator-only exam

Who: Natalia Sidarova, computer scientist, Eindhoven University of Technology (Netherlands).

What they did: Data-analytics take-home assignments now receive only pass or fail, and AI-generated code is permitted. The final exam allows only basic calculators.

What happened: "We've never had as many students achieving full-score grades in the exams as we did this year," she told Nature.

What you could borrow: Decouple the practice from the grade, so AI use during practice carries no incentive, and let the exam carry the weight.
