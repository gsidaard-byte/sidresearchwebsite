🎓 Higher Ed · 📊 Research · Nov 4–9
Image source: Nano Banana 2

AI-written article reviews beat Harvard’s best human reviewers, its own researchers report

The brief: Gary King and Steven Worthington of Harvard’s Institute for Quantitative Social Science compared AI reviews with human reviews from rapidPeer, their own double-blind pre-submission review service, and found the AI reviews better on every measure they built (Times Higher Education, Nov 4; Inside Higher Ed, Nov 5). King says he set out to show the value of human review and will now have to “pivot what the organisation does.”

The details:

  • AI beat the human review in 100% of pairs when judged by another AI model and in 87% when judged by human journal editors, with higher ratings in all seven areas assessed. The working paper is not yet peer reviewed.
  • King credits AI with removing “sycophancy” between reviewers and authors, but the paper warns AI may push science toward “homogenous arguments and conclusions.”
  • Separate tests point the same way on error-finding: with 100 errors planted in 10 psychology papers, the best AI system caught 71 and all systems pooled caught 93 (Litvak, arXiv, Nov 9). In a clinical-trial version, one of 15 LLMs caught nearly all planted errors; human reviewers in a 2008 test found 27% (Science, Nov 9).
Worth noting: Critics say rapidPeer reviewers are told not to judge relevance or interest, so the comparison tests quality control rather than the whole job of a peer, and that the authors run the service they evaluated (The Geyser, Nov 6; Scholarly Kitchen, Nov 5). A replication on journal reviews that include novelty and significance judgments would show how far the result travels.

What this means for your campus

Faculty will ask whether they may use AI on reviews they owe journals and funders; check each venue’s policy first, since many still prohibit it.

🏫 K-12 · ⚖️ Policy · Nov 3–7
Image source: Nano Banana 2

Google switches AI off by default for K-12 students, and ties Search to it

The brief: Google says Gemini, Gemini Notebook, Gemini in Classroom and Google Search will be “entirely off for K-12 student accounts until school administrators explicitly turn them on,” in a November 3 post by education VP Chris Phillips (AP, Nov 4). Phillips told the New York Times that schools rejecting its AI “will not have access to search,” which drew immediate backlash.

The details:

  • Later the same day Google said schools can enable Search alone, but Search will still carry AI features such as AI-generated summaries (NBC News, Nov 4).
  • Guided Learning, which walks students through problems rather than giving answers, becomes the default chatbot in Google Classroom for schools that turn Gemini on (Chalkbeat, Nov 4). Google has not set a date for the new defaults.
  • New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani: “Google is trying to use its monopoly power to force AI into our public schools” (CBS News, Nov 3). City officials have already disabled Gemini and AI Mode on student Chromebooks (SILive, citing Gothamist, Nov 7).
  • The move reverses Google’s August decision to turn Gemini on for many under-18 students. AFT president Randi Weingarten said Google “has prioritized its business interests over the education of our children” (PC Gamer, Nov 3).

🌍 Global · 🎓 Higher Ed · Ireland · Nov 6–7
Image source: Nano Banana 2

Nearly 9 in 10 Irish academics report AI problems in student work

The brief: At a November 6 conference at Technological University Dublin, Ireland’s two lecturer unions said improper AI use threatens confidence in the country’s third-level qualifications (Irish Times, Nov 7). In their survey, 54% of academics said they meet AI-related issues frequently and 33% occasionally.

The details:

  • 83% of surveyed academics said they still have concerns about academic integrity and plagiarism.
  • IFUT’s Frank Jones and TUI’s Michael Gillespie said detection tools or bans alone are no longer enough; the priority is rethinking assessment so students genuinely acquire disciplinary knowledge (RTÉ, Nov 6).
  • Technological University of the Shannon described a tiered model running from prohibition to full engagement, set by the outcomes each course wants.
  • Some institutions have considered letting students use AI freely if they can show they understand the results; University College Cork’s James O’Sullivan asked: “Who is going to pay for all of that?”

🎓 Higher Ed · 🛠️ Tools · California · Nov 4
Image source: Nano Banana 2

A California university hands MBA lectures to AI avatars of its professors

The brief: San Francisco Bay University has given lecturing duties in its online MBA Pro+ to AI avatars of human faculty and plans more “Pro+” programs (Chronicle of Higher Education, Nov 4). Its leaders project thousands of enrollees within a few years.

The details:

  • SFBU has spent close to $2 million on the program, which its leaders do not expect to turn a profit for a while.
  • The avatars are programmed to be “Socratic,” work in 50 languages, and are shaped by faculty; an adjunct job listing says instructors must “regularly train and tune the AI course avatar and curriculum materials.”
  • SFBU’s Ladany, an avowed critic of mainstream higher ed: “Most faculty aren’t very good at the lectures.” The university says the avatars “amplify and extend” faculty rather than replace them.
Worth noting: The university says the avatars “grade without bias”; that is its own claim, and the Chronicle reports no learning-outcome data yet. Comparing MBA Pro+ completion and assessment results with SFBU’s conventional MBA would test it.

From the Research

Randomized trial · Frontiers in Psychology · Oct 30

AI feedback improved essays as much as teacher feedback, but motivated students less

What was studied: 312 undergraduates (287 analyzed) were randomly assigned to AI feedback, teacher feedback or no substantive feedback on an argumentative essay; researchers measured revision quality and motivation. Published Oct 30, indexed this week.

What they found: Both kinds of feedback improved revisions about equally (d = 0.60 for AI, 0.59 for teachers), and engagement with the feedback explained the gain. Teacher feedback produced higher intrinsic motivation, linked to students feeling connected to the source. Caveat: one revision cycle at one institution.

What you could do with this: Let AI handle line-level comments, and add a few sentences of your own so students know a person read their work.

Evidence review · Stanford SCALE · Nov 10
Update from the Oct 27 issue

Rigorous causal studies of AI in K-12 rose from 20 to 52; results still inconsistent

What was studied: Stanford’s SCALE Initiative updated its review of AI-in-education research; its repository grew from 818 papers in March to 1,686 by the end of June.

What they found: Causal studies rose from 20 to 52, but AI’s effects on learning remain inconsistent. Teacher AI use can raise the quantity and quality of feedback, yet at least one study found it lowered student motivation and confidence (summary, Nov 10).

What you could do with this: Treat any single “AI improves learning” claim as provisional, and pilot a new tool in one section before scaling it.

How Others Are Doing It

University of Glasgow · AI and Ethics · Nov 6

A Glasgow politics lecturer turns suspected AI misuse into an “affirmation viva”

Who: Georgios Karyotis, Political and International Studies, University of Glasgow, UK.

What they did: After ChatGPT “confessed” to writing part of a student’s dissertation, the department held its first affirmation viva: a short oral meeting to check understanding, not a misconduct hearing.

What happened: The student acknowledged experimenting with chatbots and showed understanding and ownership of the work. The paper sets five principles, including a high threshold for calling a viva, a presumption of innocence, and marking only after the viva; it suggests 20–30 minutes is enough.

What you could borrow: When you doubt authorship, ask the student to explain the work before you mark or report it.
