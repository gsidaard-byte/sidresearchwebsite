🎓 Higher Ed · ⚖️ Policy · Update · Aug 5–6

Turnitin's answer to the detector retreat: watching students type

The move away from AI detectors — covered in the last two issues — reached a turning point this week. Inside Higher Ed reports Yale, Vanderbilt, Johns Hopkins, and Indiana now bar detector scores as sole evidence of cheating, and at least a dozen universities including Northwestern, Georgetown, and NYU have disabled Turnitin's AI detection entirely. A new national data point: 73% of faculty say they have personally dealt with AI-related integrity issues.

Turnitin — licensed by roughly 2,500 U.S. universities — is pivoting to Clarity, which tracks keystrokes and lets instructors replay a student's entire writing process. A study cited by the Chronicle put its text detector at 70–80% accuracy. Indiana's Kelley School now prohibits detectors outright: "Instead of trying to 'catch' AI use, focus on designing assignments that encourage process, reasoning, and authentic engagement."

Worth noting: Turnitin claims high accuracy on unedited AI text; independent findings are far weaker on mixed human-AI writing. The open question is whether writing replay is integrity protection or surveillance students will reject — the same wrongful-flag dynamic behind the Yale lawsuit covered August 1.

Chronicle, Aug 6 →   Inside Higher Ed, Aug 5 →
What this means for your campus

Before you build a fall integrity case on a detector score, confirm what your institution now accepts as evidence — the ground shifted this summer. And expect Clarity-style writing replay to arrive as the vendor's proposed alternative, with its own consent questions.

🛠️ Tools · 🎓 Higher Ed · Aug 4

OpenAI ships education plugins for faculty and students

Three plugins for ChatGPT Work and Codex connect the chatbot to course materials, documents, and calendars. College Educator handles syllabus updates, multimedia assessments, and LMS packaging; College Student turns course materials into guided tutoring, study guides, quizzes, and flashcards. OpenAI claims a student "capability overhang" — advanced users tapping 90–99% less capability than power users — without explaining the methodology. Early reactions are company-gathered; The Register argues the launch reads the room badly amid AI-enabled cheating.

For your campus: if you run ChatGPT Edu, preview these against your own assignments before your students do.

🎓 Higher Ed · 📊 Research · Aug 4

Student AI use up fivefold — and students want norms

American University's Kogod School of Business finds regular student AI use rose from 6.2% to 29% in three years. Nearly half of students are concerned about academic integrity; 40% in a companion Inside Higher Ed survey worry AI is eroding independent thinking. Axios notes Wharton research finding people generate a broader range of ideas with their own thinking plus web search than with ChatGPT.

For your campus: clear, per-assignment AI rules are now a student demand, not just a compliance chore.

Axios, Aug 4 →
🏫 K-12 · 🛠️ Tools · Aug 4

Gemini in Classroom opens to students of all ages on August 10

Google Classroom's Gemini tab — flashcards, practice quizzes, and study guides generated from actual course materials, synced to Gemini Notebook — extends from 18-plus higher-ed students to students of all ages wherever admins have enabled Gemini. It ships on every Workspace for Education tier including free Fundamentals. Admins can carve out under-18 org units, but where it's on, it's on automatically. Your incoming classes will have studied with AI built into their LMS since middle school — assessment designs that assume unaided study habits are aging out fast.

From the Research

Cognitive Offloading · Quasi-Experimental

Bounded AI support beats open collaboration — with effect sizes

An eight-week classroom study compared students writing with stage-bounded AI support against open AI collaboration (112 AI-exposed students). By week eight, bounded support led on writing quality (+0.27), higher-order thinking (+0.35), argument depth (+0.42), and independent revision (+0.39). Open collaborators prompted more, self-generated less, and offloaded more reasoning. This extends the July 21 finding that deeper "collaboration" doesn't mean deeper learning — now with a design answer: structure AI permission by stage, not by assignment.

Frontiers in Psychology, Aug 5 →
Student Perception · Survey

Complement or replacement? The frame students hold matters

University students who see AI as a complement to their own thinking report consistently higher cognitive independence than those who see it as a replacement. Authors flag it as hypothesis-generating — small sample, self-report. The practical read: describe AI in your syllabus as a thinking partner to argue with, not a substitute, and ask students to reflect on which one it was.

How Others Are Doing It

Instructor-Side AI · Elon University

Amanda Sturgill audits the instructor's side of the desk

Writing for Elon's Center for Engaged Learning, Sturgill catalogued the hidden risks of instructors using GenAI for grading, feedback, and materials — hypocrisy when students are barred from the same tools, automation bias, feedback that reads generic and lazy. She tested the workflow herself: a 20-minute podcast generated from her 750-word article contained fabricated details in the first minute. Borrow: disclose your own AI use, verify every artifact, hold yourself to your syllabus standard.

Center for Engaged Learning, Aug 4 →
Assessment Design · r/Teaching

Kill the incentive, co-write the rules

In an r/teaching thread, one instructor removed performance grades from written assignments — full credit for submission, feedback in exchange for skipping AI. Others negotiated class-level AI compacts. Students report classmates "genuinely trying to follow the rules" when they helped set them. Borrow: separate writing-for-feedback from writing-for-grades, and spend one session co-writing your AI rules — buy-in tracks authorship.

Reddit, Aug 2 →
Talk About It — Questions for Your Department

If our institution no longer accepts detector scores as evidence, what is our standard for a fall AI-misconduct case — process artifacts, oral defenses, or something else?

Turnitin Clarity can replay a student's keystrokes. Would we ask students to write under that kind of monitoring — and what would we say when they ask why we don't trust them?
