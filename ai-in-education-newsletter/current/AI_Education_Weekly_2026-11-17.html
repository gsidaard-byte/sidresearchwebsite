🌍 Global · ⚖️ Policy · England · Nov 11
Image source: Nano Banana 2

England’s regulator tells universities to move toward secure, AI-free assessment

The brief: On November 11 the Office for Students said universities must ensure assessments are valid and reliable measures of what students know, and that “in many cases” this will require secure, AI-free assessment (OfS, Nov 11). It issued guidance applying its existing quality conditions to AI, alongside a joint report with Advance HE, Making AI Work for Students (Advance HE, Nov 11).

The details:

  • The research drew on about 300 students and staff at more than 120 institutions over four months. It found many unsupervised assessments can be completed with AI, and that neither human markers nor detection tools reliably identify AI-generated text. It cites student AI use for assessments at 94% in late 2025, up from 53% in late 2023 (OfS/Advance HE report, Nov 11; Wonkhe).
  • “Secure” means formats designed to prevent AI use: in-person exams, presentations, oral exams, performances, and practical or applied tasks.
  • OfS strategy director Josh Fleming called AI a “generational risk to the system as it stands” (The Guardian, Nov 11). Providers that fall short could face monetary penalties or suspension of OfS grant funding, and the Department for Education said it expects the OfS to “enforce the existing standards effectively” (Telegraph via Yahoo, Nov 11). No redesign deadline was set.
  • Universities UK said many institutions have already made “substantial changes” and that “different subjects will require different approaches” (Times Higher Education, Nov 11).
Worth noting: Times Higher Education reports the guidance stops short of mandating AI-free assessment, while the OfS release says it will often be required. How OfS handles its first case against a provider that relied on unsupervised coursework will show which reading holds.

What this means for your campus

🎓 Higher Ed · 📊 Research · Nov 17
Image source: Nano Banana 2

A quarter of undergraduates say they now write to dodge AI detectors

The brief: In Inside Higher Ed’s main Student Voice survey of 5,034 undergraduates at 310 mostly public institutions, 25% said they have changed how they write to avoid being falsely flagged by AI detectors (Inside Higher Ed, Nov 17). Eight in 10 use generative or agentic AI routinely.

The details:

  • Top effects students report: studying more efficiently (34%), more confidence with hard concepts (33%), relying less on professors or tutoring centers (30%), and relying on AI too heavily to think or write on their own (16%).
  • Asked what responsible AI use means, 65% said helping them learn rather than doing the work for them, and 60% said transparency about when and how AI is used.
  • The survey was fielded in October; 20% said they do not use AI for college work.
  • UCLA’s Tina Austin: students are “distinguishing between AI just being there as a productivity tool and completing their work, and actually coming home and feeling like they learned something.”

What this means for your campus

If a detector is part of your process, one in four students may be writing worse on purpose to avoid it; tell them how you will and won’t use detection.

🎓 Higher Ed · California · Announced Nov 3, covered through Nov 13
Image source: Nano Banana 2

Stanford puts $10 million into redesigning whole majors, not single courses

The brief: Stanford’s provost is funding a $10 million initiative for departments to rethink undergraduate majors “from introductory courses through capstones” for the AI era (Stanford Report, Nov 3; Forbes, Nov 11). Applications open in winter quarter 2027.

The details:

  • Rolling one- to two-year grants of $250,000 to $750,000, expected to support about 15 departments or programs through 2028–29. (Stanford’s release gives the $750,000 ceiling; the $250,000 floor comes from press coverage.)
  • Run by the Vice Provost for Undergraduate Education’s AI Meets Education at Stanford effort with the Stanford Accelerator for Learning.
  • The driving question: what should students “learn, demonstrate, and do on their own when AI tools can write, code, and create at their behest?”
  • Proposals may include ways to assess learning without AI, such as oral assessments and in-class work.

What this means for your campus

Course-by-course fixes are giving way to program-level redesign; ask your chair whether your major has mapped where students build and show skills without AI.

From the Research

Evidence synthesis · Harvard GSE · Nov 12

Harvard’s Compass Collaborative turns 5,000 studies into age-specific AI guidance

What was studied: Researchers at the Harvard Graduate School of Education reviewed more than 5,000 education, psychology and human-development studies to build the AI Guidance by Age matrix, launched the week of November 12.

What they found: Users pick a role (including higher-education educator), a developmental domain and an age range; the tool returns best uses, assessment advice and likely help-or-hinder effects. For 18- to 25-year-olds in language and literacy, it advises using AI to “widen what a young adult reads, reflect their writing back to them,” and to “leave the composing and the puzzling-out” to the student. Caveat: much of the underlying evidence predates generative AI.

What you could do with this: Run your course’s age range and domain through the matrix and compare its advice with your current AI policy.

Randomized experiment · arXiv · Oct 2026 version

AI helps students learn when it tutors, not when it writes

What was studied: Middlebury economists Zara Contractor and Germán Reyes randomly gave about 210 undergraduates access to generative AI while writing an essay on an unfamiliar topic, then tested them unaided right away and a week later (preregistration). Not new this week, and not covered in previous issues.

What they found: AI access raised test scores, with a smaller gain a week later. The gains held for students who used AI as a tutor and faded for those who had AI write for them.

What you could do with this: Allow AI for questioning and feedback on drafts, but require students to compose the text themselves.

How Others Are Doing It

University of Chicago · Inside Higher Ed · Nov 9

UChicago will make an AI-free writing course mandatory for all first-years

Who: The new Chicago Center for Writing at the University of Chicago, led by executive director Abigail Reardon (UChicago News).

What they did: Launched the center in late October. Starting next fall, every first-year must take an AI-free writing course run through it. The center is also building labs where students and faculty can “experiment with the practices and technologies of writing, both old and new.”

What happened: Too early for outcomes; the course begins in fall 2027.

What you could borrow: Protect one early, foundational writing experience from AI, and tell students explicitly when AI-assisted writing comes later.

Source →
Eastern Illinois University · Petition & Student Senate

Eastern Illinois students ask the university to regulate faculty AI use

Who: Students and the Student Senate at Eastern Illinois University (petition; Daily Eastern News, Oct 11).

What they did: A petition (529 signatures as of Nov 8) argues that while students face AI rules, “we have nothing preventing professors from using AI as a substitute for learning materials.” The Student Senate then passed a resolution asking administrators to regulate faculty AI use.

What happened: Organizers don’t want a ban; they want professors to disclose when and how they use AI, and the university to address the faculty workloads behind it. No administrative response has been reported.

What you could borrow: Ask students which kinds of faculty AI use they’d want disclosed, then publish your own norm in the syllabus.

Talk About It

Tied to: detectors & the OfS report

One in four students say they write differently to avoid detector flags, and England’s regulator says detectors don’t reliably work. Should our department stop treating detector scores as evidence, and say so publicly?

Tied to: Student Voice & EIU petition

60% of students say responsible AI use means transparency, and EIU students want faculty AI use regulated. Should our department agree on what faculty disclose about AI in course materials and grading?
