⚖️ Policy · 🎓 Higher Ed · Aug 30

Universities are policing student AI use while leaving their own admissions offices ungoverned

Student Defense's SHAPE AI Initiative filed public records requests with 24 public colleges and universities in spring 2026 asking for policies governing AI use in undergraduate admissions. The report, released August 30, found most institutions have none — even as admissions offices adopt AI for reading applications the report calls "high-stakes, individualized, and legally sensitive."

Chief Counsel Dan Zibel: "too many colleges and universities have yet to establish the policies and trainings needed to ensure AI is used responsibly in admissions offices." The report recommends human accountability for decisions, disclosure to applicants, a bar on undisclosed staff AI use, and applicant-data protection. The gap mirrors student-facing integrity policy in reverse: institutions demand disclosure from students that they do not practice institutionally.

What this means for your campus

If your institution requires students to disclose AI use, expect the same question to be turned back on admissions, advising, and grading — and it will come from journalists or lawyers before it comes from faculty senate.

🎓 Higher Ed · 🛠️ Tools · Update

Update on the back-to-school giveaways (Aug 22 issue): OpenAI adds four free months of ChatGPT Plus

After Google's free year for students, OpenAI opened four free monthly billing periods of Plus — an $80 value — for verified U.S. college students, claimable through October 31 via SheerID. Marc Watkins (University of Mississippi) notes students now hold simultaneous free premium trials from OpenAI, xAI, and Google, calls the equity effects "a staggering blow," and warns that assessments vetted against free-tier ChatGPT no longer describe what students actually have. Assume premium AI in your classroom until at least December.

📊 Research · 🎓 Higher Ed · Aug 25

Nature: AI detectors quietly got much better — and Chicago is using one on coursework

A Nature news feature reports the new detector generation — Pangram most prominently — correctly identifies human writing as human "almost all the time" in independent analyses, a reversal of the false-positive problem. The University of Chicago has started using Pangram to vet coursework; alphaXiv shows verdicts on arXiv preprints. Worth noting: this cuts against MIT's advice (Aug 26 issue) not to rely on detectors, and peer-reviewed accuracy data across diverse student writing does not yet exist.

🎓 Higher Ed · 🏫 K-12 · Aug 27 · U.S.

Teacher-education programs get their first national AI framework

AACTE released a framework for how university teacher-preparation programs should teach AI — technical skills, pedagogical knowledge, and ethical judgment — developed by a task force of members and outside experts. Until now most teacher AI training came from districts and vendors, often platform-specific; co-chair Peña Bedesem (Kent State) and Anne Tapp Jaksa (Michigan) argue AI now touches the fundamentals candidates learn: planning, assessment, differentiation, ethics. If your institution prepares teachers, this is the document your accreditation and curriculum committees will be handed this year.

From the Research

Faculty practice · Engineering

"It's here and we can't fight it" — engineering faculty are improvising without guidance

A Frontiers in Education study finds adoption sharply uneven: some faculty redesign assignments around AI while same-department colleagues prohibit it — and both report the same root condition: policy, training, and pedagogy haven't kept pace. The takeaway is an argument for department-level norms: the inconsistency students complain about lives between adjacent classrooms.

Frontiers, Aug 28 →
Conceptual · Higher-ed futures

What universities are for when AI makes knowledge cheap

A conceptual review proposes the "Human-Agency Infrastructure Framework": when explanations become abundant, the scarce goods shift to judgment, trust, credible evidence, belonging, and ethical agency. Useful curriculum-committee frame: ask which scarce goods each course actually builds, not just what students may use AI for.

How Others Are Doing It

Adam Grant · Wharton, UPenn

He swapped his exam for an essay on why students cheat

Tipped off that students planned to use AI on a take-home exam, Grant replaced it with one essay: use the organizational psychology you've studied to design a way to motivate students not to cheat. His survey of 95 students first: hardly any saw cheating as an integrity violation. His conclusion — obstacles don't work; change motivation, not surveillance. Borrow the move: make the integrity problem itself the assessed content.

NYT, Aug 26 →
Jason Gibson · Alcorn State (reported secondhand)

A hidden prompt measured unedited AI use: 32 of 35 students

Gibson embedded hidden instructions in a writing prompt — triggered only if students pasted it into a chatbot and submitted the output unread. 91% turned in gibberish about Madagascar. The diagnostic matters more than the trap: the problem is not AI use but zero editorial oversight — a teachable distinction. (Primary post not independently located; details as reported.)
