🎓 Higher Ed · ⚖️ Policy · Oct 27–31

Update on the Dartmouth provost story from the Sept 23 and 29 issues: he resigned, and the evidence came from his campus Claude logs, not a detector

View generated image — Image source: Nano Banana 2

Provost Santiago Schnell resigned on October 28 after the independent faculty-and-alumni committee found he "used AI to meaningfully alter his writing" and "fell short" of Dartmouth's academic integrity standards. This answers the test we set in September: the committee did publish findings, and they rest on logs and publisher policies rather than Pangram scores.

  • The committee's eight-page report (Oct 27) says it used no AI detectors. It reviewed his Dartmouth-issued laptop, his emails and his enterprise Claude account.
  • The logs show he used Claude to "substantially edit his research manuscript, troubleshoot errors in computer code, and generate figures" after peer review for a paper in ChemSystemsChem, whose policies required disclosure. He disclosed nothing.
  • He did not hand over logs from personal AI accounts and did not meet the committee. His lawyer now says he was not given a "meaningful opportunity" to respond.
  • He resigned less than two weeks before a planned faculty no-confidence vote. Historian Matthew Delmont is interim provost through June 2028.
Worth noting: The committee relied on chat logs stored by the campus AI tool. Neither Dartmouth nor its faculty have said publicly whether students' and employees' campus AI logs can be used the same way in other misconduct cases. A written policy on who may access those logs, and when, would settle it.

🎓 Higher Ed · 📊 Research · Nov 2

Nearly 9 in 10 faculty say AI has damaged their confidence that student work is real

View generated image — Image source: Nano Banana 2

In Inside Higher Ed's annual faculty survey with Hanover Research, professors named "the increased use of generative and agentic AI" as their top concern about higher education, ahead of politics. 1,665 faculty responded (Sept 21–Oct 8; ±2.4 points). Nearly nine in 10 say AI has hurt their confidence in the authenticity of students' work; 67% say it has hurt trust with students. Only 18% say AI has helped them give timely or individualized support. AAC&U's José Antonio Bowen: faculty "are seeing students use AI in ways that undermine the learning process and also the trust in the work."

What this means for your campus: If AI tools aren't saving you time on student support, faculty development should focus on rebuilding trust in student work, not on adoption.

🎓 Higher Ed · 🛠️ Tools · Oct 29

UT Austin's contracts put a price on campus-wide AI: about $4.2 million for ChatGPT Edu and Claude

View generated image — Image source: Nano Banana 2

Contracts obtained by The Daily Texan show ChatGPT Edu costs about $1.1 million a year for 75,000 users, and Claude for Education is a two-year, $2 million commitment for 70,000 users (chat, Cowork and Claude Code), plus a $73,000 premium tier. About 18,000 accounts have been created across the two tools since June. The paper reports UT is among at least four universities, with UChicago, Stanford and Emory, that signed Claude for Education since June without appearing on Anthropic's public list.

What this means for your campus: Ask your CIO what your campus pays per seat and how many people actually use the tools; those two numbers frame every "should we buy more AI" conversation.

⚖️ Policy · 🎓 Higher Ed · Nov 2

A University of Kentucky administrator gets a religious exemption from using AI at work

View generated image — Image source: Nano Banana 2

The University of Kentucky granted research administrator David Durman, a humanist, a religious accommodation under Title VII so he does not have to use AI tools in his job. He asked after the university began asking employees to build AI into their workflows. The request was granted in September; Religion News Service reported it on October 26, and the American Humanist Association, which backed him, publicized it. Durman told RNS that offloading his thinking "is undercutting my ability to be a moral actor." It is an administrative accommodation, not a court ruling, so it sets no binding precedent.

[Unverified]: The AHA calls this the first known case of a secular belief winning an exemption from AI use. The claim comes from the advocacy group and secondary reports; we found no independent count.

What this means for your campus: If your institution is starting to expect AI use in staff or faculty work, HR will need an accommodation process; ask whether one exists.

From the Research

Student survey · Dartmouth AI Survey · Oct 30

Students think their classmates use AI to cut corners far more than they do

What was studied: The inaugural Dartmouth AI Survey, led by economist Bruce Sacerdote and engineer Peter Chin, surveyed more than 1,500 Dartmouth students and about 1,000 U.S. college students between June and September.

What they found: 11% say they use AI to solve problem sets, but they think 49% of peers do; 8% say they paste AI text with little editing, but they think 50% of peers do. 70% say take-home work has become unfair because of AI, and 39% feel pressure to use it because peers do. 84% nationally (92% at Dartmouth) say AI can hurt writing skills. Field mattered more than class year: 70% of Dartmouth science students say AI deepens understanding, vs. 48% in arts and humanities. Caveat: self-reports, and no full methods report yet.

What you could do with this: Students' inflated beliefs about peers' shortcuts may feed the 39% who feel pressured to use AI. Showing a class the real self-reported numbers, or collecting your own anonymously, may ease that pressure.

How Others Are Doing It

University of Manchester, UK · Chemical Engineering · Oct 30

Do, then check, then defend: an engineering lecturer's AI sequence

Who: Salman Shahid, senior lecturer in chemical engineering and discipline head of education at the University of Manchester.

What he did: Students first frame the problem and state assumptions without AI. Then they use AI under a limiting prompt ("Act as an engineering tutor. Do not give me the final answer. Review my proposed approach, identify inappropriate assumptions…"). Next they check the AI's output for units, assumptions and physical sense; sometimes he hands out an AI solution with a planted error. Finally, groups peer-review each other as "engineering reviewers" and defend their decisions.

What happened: No measured student outcomes. His rationale: in his own testing, ChatGPT was about 95% correct on recall questions but about 41% on open-ended problems needing professional judgement. "A student who does not understand a material balance cannot reliably identify an incorrect AI-generated material balance."

What you could borrow: Make students attempt the problem before they see any AI output, then grade the critique, not the answer.
