Propeller Aerodynamics and Distributed Propulsion

These papers track how propeller performance changes once the rotor is installed in a real configuration. The core issue is coupling: nearby ground, ceilings, wings, neighboring rotors, pitch changes, and flight attitude all reshape the inflow and wake, so isolated-rotor assumptions quickly become incomplete. Across this body of work, the papers move from boundary-proximity effects to tandem layouts, wing-embedded propulsion, and variable-pitch control. The common thread is that wake interaction is not a side effect. It is the mechanism that determines whether a configuration loses efficiency, gains authority, or becomes predictable enough for design use.

Morphing Wings and Lift Distribution

This theme collects papers that change the lifting surface itself or redistribute load across multiple lifting surfaces so the flow does more of the work before the aircraft gets there. The common thread is not just aerodynamic improvement, but control over where lift is produced, how quickly it is shifted, and how that shift changes the wake, induced drag, and system-level performance. The papers range from passive spanloading experiments to mechanically actuated morphing concepts and multi-wing configurations intended to approximate or exploit nonuniform lift distributions.

Understanding Spray Drift

These papers study how liquid structures form, deform, and disperse when exposed to complex aerodynamic environments. The thread runs from learning-based atomization classification to spray distortion in propeller wakes and finally to fundamental breakup physics, so the category spans both applied agricultural spray transport and the underlying multiphase flow mechanisms that shape it. Taken together, the work builds a practical picture of drift: nozzle geometry sets the initial droplet population, surrounding flow reshapes that population downstream, and measurement strategy strongly affects what can be inferred about the spray field. The result is a theme that connects instrumentation, flow interaction, and application-level consequences rather than treating spray drift as a single isolated phenomenon.

Flow Diagnostics and Event-Based Sensing

This category brings together papers whose main contribution is measurement rather than configuration: new ways to see, sample, or reconstruct the flow field. Across the set, the common thread is improving what can be resolved in time, space, and signal quality, especially when the flow is noisy, highly unsteady, or difficult to instrument with conventional frame-based tools. The papers span event-based cameras, PIV variants, lidar, pressure-sensitive paint, piezoelectric wake sensing, and purpose-built flow testbeds. Taken together, the papers show a shift from single-instrument demonstrations to measurement systems that are compared, stress-tested, and adapted for harder environments. Several papers focus on algorithmic robustness for event-camera velocimetry, while others validate alternative diagnostics against known wind-tunnel and water-tunnel cases. A smaller but important subset uses embedded sensors or optical methods to infer wake structure indirectly, which is useful when direct access to the flow is limited.

Wing Aerodynamics and Wake Physics

This category traces how wings create, reshape, and shed vorticity under passive and active interventions. Across the papers, geometry changes, surface treatments, icing, boundary-layer devices, and blowing are used to probe the same underlying question: how do local flow structures translate into lift, drag, stall margin, and overall efficiency? The common thread is that aerodynamic performance is treated as a wake problem, not just a force-balance result. Several papers compare near-wake and far-wake signatures across modified wings, while others show how a change at the surface, trailing edge, or leading edge propagates downstream into vortex strength, shear-layer structure, and turbulent mixing. That makes the category useful as a bridge between design intuition and measurable flow physics.

Wake Characteristics of Bell-Shaped Lift Distribution

These papers focus on a specific question in lifting-surface aerodynamics: how does changing the spanwise lift distribution alter the wake, especially near the wingtip? The set centers on bell-shaped and other non-elliptical load distributions, including Prandtl-D3 and Prandtl-D3C configurations, and shows that wake behavior is not a simple byproduct of total lift. Instead, the spanwise loading itself changes where the vorticity rolls up, how strongly the tip vortex persists, and how the near wake transitions into the far wake. Taken together, the papers provide an experimentally grounded view of induced-drag management through load shaping. They connect lift distribution to wake topology, demonstrating that bell-shaped loading can redistribute circulation away from the wingtip and weaken the conventional tip-vortex structure. That makes the category important not only as a wake-physics problem, but also as a design pathway for low-induced-drag wings, distributed-lift concepts, and unconventional configurations that aim to control the wake rather than merely tolerate it.

Gust Encounters and Closed-Loop Aerodynamic Control

These papers trace a focused line of inquiry into how unsteady inflow changes aerodynamic loads and how feedback can be used to shape that response. The theme now spans propeller gust response, lift regulation, gust mitigation, and powered-wing response studies, so the theme is not just disturbance rejection but the broader problem of making aerodynamic systems predictable in unsteady air. The result is a coherent body of work on unsteady aerodynamics that is as much about sensing and actuation as it is about the flow itself.

Experimental Platforms, Education, and Cross-Domain Fluid Systems

This category collects papers whose main contribution is the experimental, pedagogical, or cross-domain infrastructure that makes fluid-dynamics research possible. The papers span wind-tunnel characterization, additive-manufactured facilities, wind-lens and lensed-turbine studies, and aerodynamics teaching models. Read together, they show a program that treats experimentation itself as a scientific asset: the facility, the measurement approach, and the application domain all matter to the final result. The common thread is capability-building. Some papers validate a platform so later unsteady-flow work can be trusted. Others transfer aerodynamic tools into wind-energy settings, showing that wake analysis remains useful when the geometry moves away from canonical wings and propellers. The pedagogy papers fit the same pattern at a different scale: they build human infrastructure by making aerodynamics easier to understand and teach.
