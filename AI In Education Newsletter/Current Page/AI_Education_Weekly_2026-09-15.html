🎓 Higher Ed · Australia · Sept 11–15, 2026

Sydney and UNSW sign campus-wide ChatGPT Edu deals — and get immediate backlash

Image source: Nano Banana 2

The brief: The University of Sydney and UNSW Sydney each signed multi-year enterprise agreements with OpenAI this week, giving a combined 150,000+ students and staff free access to ChatGPT Edu. Both are pairing the rollout with "AI modes" — can, can't, must — that tell students exactly when AI is permitted on an assessment.

  • Sydney is investing AU$1 million in an AI Sustainability Research and Innovation Fund alongside the deal, and says ChatGPT Edu will complement, not replace, its existing AI platform, Cogniti. (Sydney Morning Herald, Sept 15)
  • UNSW says AI detection and watermarking tools "aren't yet reliable enough alone" to determine misconduct, and will support — not replace — instructor judgment. (iTnews, Sept 11)
  • Sydney's "two-lane" model — secure supervised assessments in one lane, open AI-permitted work in the other — is cited by the NYT as a model other institutions are watching. (NYT, Sept 15)
  • Staff get access this month; students follow a pilot, per student paper Honi Soit. (InfoSec Today, Sept 14)
Worth notingReaction has been sharply negative in places — Sydney union members struck two weeks ago partly over the university's "extraordinarily cavalier" approach to AI, and social comments on the announcement called it "despicable garbage." The tension between top-down enterprise deals and grassroots faculty consent is unresolved.
Read the Sydney announcement →
What this means for your campus

If your institution is weighing a campus-wide AI vendor deal, the Sydney/UNSW rollout is the closest thing to a real-time case study of both the assessment redesign and the faculty pushback that follows. Watch how they handle the "must use AI" lane — that's the piece nobody has done at scale yet.

⚖️ Policy · U.S. · Sept 15

Microsoft commits to AI privacy rules for students; OpenAI and Anthropic in talks, Google silent

Image source: Nano Banana 2

The brief: Microsoft agreed to guardrails and privacy standards for its AI in K-12 schools, negotiated with the American Federation of Teachers — including a commitment not to use student or educator data to train its models. OpenAI and Anthropic say they're negotiating similar pacts; Google, the dominant K-12 edtech provider, hasn't said whether it will.

  • Follows the NYC/LA moratoriums covered last issue — Microsoft is the first major vendor to respond with written commitments rather than an audit promise.
  • The AFT's National Academy for AI Instruction launched a year ago with $23 million from Microsoft, OpenAI, and Anthropic combined.
  • Microsoft's own third annual AI in Education report: 9 in 10 students and educators have used AI for schoolwork, but 77% of students and 53% of educators report zero formal training. (Complete AI Training, Sept 12)
  • Anthropic's September threat report separately disclosed state-linked hackers breached an ed-tech company and exfiltrated hundreds of megabytes of student data. (Anthropic, Sept 14)
What this means for your campusA K-12 agreement, but the same vendors sell into higher ed under the same corporate privacy policies. Worth checking whether your enterprise agreement contains comparable training-data commitments — K-12 pressure often becomes the template for what higher ed can negotiate next.

🎓 Higher Ed / 📊 Research · Sept 15

NYT: universities sound warnings about AI even as they embrace it

Image source: Nano Banana 2

The brief: A New York Times feature maps the split running through higher ed leadership: administrators — including Dartmouth's Sian Leah Beilock — pushing AI adoption as an "existential requirement," while a recent MIT report on "cognitive surrender" and skeptical faculty pull the other way.

  • University of Hawaii president Wendy Hensel: "You have to lean in, but you have to do so in a way that's thoughtful and ethical… The right answer to AI is not just 'no.'"
  • Turnitin's chief product officer Annie Chechitelli says many educators "don't even know where to start" on policy.
  • Same tension at Harvard: economist David Deming's first-day email endorsing "AI encouragement" drew accusations that Harvard was "surrendering to techno-determinism." (Derek Thompson, Sept 11)
  • Corroborating data: in Anthropic's survey of 81,000 Claude users, more than half of students reported learning benefits — but 16% also noted signs of cognitive atrophy in themselves, topped only by teachers (24%) and academics (19%). (Anthropic)
What this means for your campusThe "encourage vs. restrict" debate is not settling into consensus. Expect pressure from your administration to have a clear, public position before this becomes a student-facing controversy on your own campus.

🛠️ Tools / 📊 Research · Sept 8–15

AI detectors are quietly pivoting from cheating enforcement to assessment audits

Image source: Nano Banana 2

The brief: A growing number of universities are backing away from using AI-detection scores as grounds for misconduct charges, and instead using detection tools to audit whether an assignment can still measure genuine understanding at all.

  • An independent University of Chicago comparison found Pangram the most accurate major text detector — but even a claimed 99.98% can't rule out false positives on individual student cases. (Gizmodo, Sept 9)
  • Turnitin states plainly that its AI Writing Report "may misidentify both human and AI-generated writing and should not be the sole basis for action against a student."
  • MarketScale: detection is being repositioned "from academic integrity enforcement to audit tools for assessment." (MarketScale, Sept 8)
  • MUSC's five-tier framework (see Research & Practice) is one concrete alternative institutions are adopting instead.
Worth notingDartmouth's Hany Farid, who has spent years building deepfake-detection tools, says he now "mistrusts much of what he sees on a screen." Even the experts building these tools describe the arms race as unresolved.
What this means for your campusIf your syllabus still treats a detector score as proof of misconduct, this week is a good prompt to check your institution's current guidance — several peer institutions have already walked that back.

🌍 Global / 📊 Research · Latin America & Caribbean

UNESCO: 87% of Latin American and Caribbean universities use AI, only 26% have a strategy

Image source: Nano Banana 2

The brief: A UNESCO IESALC survey of 200 higher education institutions across 19 countries, launched at Digital Learning Week 2026 in Paris, found grassroots AI use has far outpaced institutional governance.

  • Only 26% of surveyed institutions have a formal AI strategy despite 87% adoption.
  • UNESCO recommends AI literacy become a core graduate competency, with expanded training and dedicated governance structures.
  • For governments: fold higher ed into national AI strategies with dedicated funding, and build AI literacy into accreditation frameworks.
  • UNESCO has opened a global consultation on AI governance in education — comments due October 15. (UNESCO)
What this means for your campusIf you have active AI tools in classrooms but no written strategy, you're the norm, not the exception — but the gap is exactly what accreditors and governments are starting to ask about.

Research & practice

What the evidence says,
and what people are actually doing

Two studies worth your time, two institutions worth borrowing from.

From the Research

Measurement · Frontiers in Education · Sept 10, 2026

A validated scale for "assessment-specific" AI literacy

What was studied: Researchers developed and validated the Generative AI Assessment Literacy Scale (GAA-LS), an 18-item instrument measuring students' capacity to use GenAI responsibly in assessment contexts, across two studies with 486 students.

What they found: Five distinct factors — including verification/evidence-checking and ethical attribution — and scores were positively associated with both feedback engagement and academic-integrity intention.

What you could do with this: The five factors are a ready-made checklist for what to actually teach in an "AI literacy" module. Verification and evidence-checking is the piece most syllabi currently skip.

Survey · Anthropic · 81,000 users · Sept 2026

Students report benefits and cognitive atrophy — at the same time

What was studied: Anthropic's interview-based survey of 81,000 Claude users, broken out by occupation, including students, teachers, and academics.

What they found: More than half of student respondents reported real learning benefits — but educators were 2.5 to 3 times more likely than average users to report witnessing cognitive atrophy, "presumably in their students."

What you could do with this: Benefit and harm are showing up in the same population. Treat "my students say AI helps them" and "I'm worried about their thinking" as compatible observations, not contradictory ones, when you talk policy with colleagues.

How Others Are Doing It

Cornell University · NSF grant · Sept 14, 2026

A Socratic AI that refuses to give the answer

Who: Alexandra Werth (biomedical engineering), with Natasha Holmes (physics) and Michelle Smith (undergraduate education), Cornell.

What they did: Won a $750,000 NSF grant to test the Automated Critical Thinking Reflection (ACTR) tool — a generative-AI system that asks probing follow-ups to make students defend their reasoning, across physics, biology, and engineering courses.

What happened: The project is just starting; Cornell has not reported whether the tool improves critical thinking, and there is no results date yet.

What you could borrow: Reframe AI-assisted assignments around Socratic follow-up questions rather than answer-checking. The pedagogical shift, not the tool, is the transferable idea.

Medical University of South Carolina · Sept 12, 2026

Five tiers instead of allow/ban

Who: Faculty and administrators at MUSC.

What they did: Replaced a binary AI policy with a five-tier "AI Acceptable Use Framework" that maps categories of acceptable use (from "no AI" to full "AI exploration") to assignment types, requiring documentation of AI use in every tier but the first.

What happened: National attention, including webinars with the Digital Education Council and AAC&U, as institutions look for alternatives to detection-based enforcement.

What you could borrow: A tiered, assignment-by-assignment framework gives students clearer guidance than a single syllabus line — and gives you a documentation trail instead of a detector score.

Talk about it — for the department meeting or the coffee line

The Sydney/UNSW deals were negotiated centrally, with faculty and students finding out largely after the fact. Should your institution negotiate an enterprise AI deal top-down, or should departments have real input before the contract is signed?

Microsoft's AFT pact commits to not training on student data. Would you want — and could you get — that same commitment in writing from every AI vendor your syllabus points students toward?
