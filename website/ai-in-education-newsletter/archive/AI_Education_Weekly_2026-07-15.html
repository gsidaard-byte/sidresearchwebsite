🎓 Higher Ed · July 8, 2026

Brown professor suspects most of his class used AI to cheat

Economics professor Roberto Serrano gave his first take-home midterm in nearly two decades; scores clustered suspiciously high. He moved the final in-person — and grades tanked. Serrano told Business Insider "the cost of cheating has basically gone down to zero," and shared details with Brown's standing committee on the academic code on July 8. The story went viral among academics, drawing 34,000+ upvotes on r/technology and a flood of instructors reporting the same take-home/in-person score gap.

The same week, Brown's Generative AI in Teaching and Learning committee published its inaugural report: three-quarters of 105 faculty respondents are concerned about AI-enabled cheating, yet the committee recommends de-emphasizing punishment and avoiding highly restrictive rules, with AI policy templates coming this summer. Worth noting: the score gap is suggestive, not proof — the final may simply have been harder, and the university investigation is ongoing.

What this means for your campus

If any of your spring assessments were take-home, this is the comparison your students — and your dean — will be making this fall. Consider running the Serrano test yourself: compare take-home and proctored score distributions before locking in your fall assessment mix.

⚖️ Policy · July 14, 2026

UChicago Law bans all tech in first-year classrooms

"Students will be there with a notebook and a pen and taking notes," Dean Adam Chilton said. The new AI strategy frames the first year as a "crucial, formative period" where "the value of effortful struggle…is paramount" — while encouraging ethical AI use for 2Ls and 3Ls. Berkeley Law went stricter in May, barring AI from any credit-bearing work across all years. Stage-based AI policy is a template your curriculum committee could adapt.

📊 Research · July 8, 2026

Global survey: 88% of students use AI — institutions haven't caught up

The Digital Education Council's 2026 global survey (45,398 responses, 35 countries) finds 88% of students and 77% of faculty using AI. But 57% of students say assessments carry inadequate AI guidance, only 29% believe instructors are equipped to guide them, and just 31% of faculty feel meaningfully involved in shaping AI policy. The adoption question is settled; the syllabus-guidance question is not.

🛠️ Tools · July 14, 2026

Anthropic launches free Claude for Teachers

Verified US K-12 educators who sign up by June 30, 2027 get a year of free premium Claude, with a teaching-skills library and curricula mapped to academic standards in all 50 states via a Learning Commons connector. Data terms are FERPA-aligned and co-developed with the American Federation of Teachers; teacher conversations aren't used for training. It joins OpenAI's ChatGPT for Teachers, Microsoft Elevate, and Google's Gemini push — and a formal pilot evaluation begins in Detroit Public Schools. For universities, the signal is clear: students arriving in 2027 will have been taught by AI-assisted teachers, and the same curriculum-aligned pitch is coming to higher ed.

What the studies say — and what colleagues are actually trying.

From the Research

Randomized Experiment · arXiv · July 9

How students use AI matters more than whether they use it

What was studied: In proctored sessions, undergraduates learned an unfamiliar topic and wrote an essay with or without generative AI, then took unaided assessments immediately and one week later (Contractor & Reyes).

What they found: Delayed learning gains appeared — but only for "augmentation" users who asked AI to explain concepts. "Automation" users who had AI generate text saw their gains vanish once AI was removed.

What you could do with this: Write assignment AI policies around the augmentation/automation distinction — permit explanation-seeking, prohibit text generation — rather than blanket allow/ban rules.

Randomized Trial · 14 Schools · July 12

Giving teachers AI didn't help students — and dented motivation

What was studied: 193 teachers and 2,816 students across 14 Turkish schools; teachers randomly received an AI teaching-support tool, the tool plus usage nudges, or neither (Süngü, Lira Luttges & Duckworth).

What they found: Teacher AI access reduced student motivation and didn't improve average performance. The median teacher conversation was just two prompts — generating outputs, not refining them.

What you could do with this: If you use AI for course prep, iterate — critique and adapt outputs rather than pasting first drafts. Productivity gains elsewhere don't transfer automatically to teaching.

How Others Are Doing It

Fudan University · Shanghai

The exam where students try to break the AI

What they did: A data-mining final where 51 students each wrote 10 questions designed to make Claude, DeepSeek, and MiniMax fail. Every question had to relate to course material, have exactly one answer, and be solved by the student first. The harder the AI failed, the higher the grade.

What happened: Nearly every student made a model fail at least once, but only four fully defeated any model — and none broke Claude entirely. Finding weaknesses required knowing the subject more deeply than the machine.

What you could borrow: A "stump the AI" assessment flips the incentive — deep domain understanding becomes the only way to score well.

University of Bath · UK

From traffic lights to two lanes

What they did: From 2026-27, Bath drops its traffic-light AI system for a principles-based "Two-Lane" approach (developed by the Association of Pacific Rim Universities): secure, supervised assessment in one lane; open AI-integrated work in the other.

What happened: It's part of a documented UK-wide shift — Cardiff, Durham, and Edinburgh Napier now require signed AI-use declarations with assessments, and several departments are reviving in-person exams (with notes allowed, not memory tests).

What you could borrow: The declaration statement is a 30-minute policy add; the two-lane framing tells you which assessments must be AI-proof and which shouldn't be.
